The Honest Number

Decimism, and forecasting between the poles

This chapter is a review — it is readable but still changing.

Two people look at the same evidence about artificial intelligence and the same future. One assigns a seventy percent probability to human extinction; the other assigns one tenth of one percent. They are not disagreeing about seven-hundred-fold worth of evidence. They are disagreeing, mostly, about which story to tell — and each has taken from the story a precision the evidence never supplied.

Making Sense of P(doom) insisted that the bare question is unanswerable until the event, the horizon, the information date, and the model are fixed, and then it declined to name a number, promising a decomposition instead: capability arrival, exposure, misalignment, loss of control, catastrophe. This chapter pays that promise. It is possible to put an honest figure on the risk — not a measurement, but a model with its inputs exposed — and when the figure is built that way it lands at neither pole. It lands in a region neither tribe has a name for, and the number, once you have it, settles less than either tribe assumes.

The Floor

Begin at the dismisser’s end, because it is the one that sounds like sobriety. A near-zero probability of catastrophe from a superintelligence is not caution; it is a prediction, and a strong one. Strip away the AI-specific intuitions and take a structurally identical case. Astronomers confirm that a superintelligent alien civilization will arrive within a few years. What is your probability that this goes badly for us? Anyone who answered one in a million would be claiming something no one could support — and showing why requires no theory of alien psychology. The aliens would be more capable than us, and we could not predict their strategies. Those two features are the whole basis of the worry, and a superintelligence shares both.

The only disanalogy is that we build the AI, and it is worth seeing why that changes less than it seems. Building the system may narrow our uncertainty about its goals; we chose its training. It cannot close our uncertainty about its strategies, because if we could reliably predict the strategies of a reasoner more capable than ourselves, the capability gap we were worried about would not exist. The chess engine is the standard reply, and it only half transfers: Stockfish has a perfectly known goal and perfectly unpredictable moves, and on the board its superiority lets you predict the one thing that matters — that you will lose. But a generally capable system does not act on a board. It acts through language, code, tools, networks, people, and institutions, where the channels are open and the moves are not enumerable in advance. So the familiar demand — name the precise failure scenario or concede there is none — misplaces the burden. A failure we can specify in advance belongs to the part of the risk we can already see; the case for taking the rest seriously never rested on that part. There is a floor below which a responsible estimate cannot go, and it is set not by any scenario but by what we cannot rule out.

The Optimist’s Symmetry

The dismisser has a sophisticated ally. Brett Hall, drawing on David Deutsch, argues that the content of future knowledge is unpredictable in principle — a knowledge-creating civilization cannot be extrapolated the way a projectile can — so confident forecasts of AI doom claim a status they cannot have. The epistemology is sound, and it is also symmetric. The same barrier that hides tomorrow’s catastrophe hides tomorrow’s cure. Openness widens the distribution of outcomes; it does not, by itself, move probability toward the good tail. Hall’s own argument leans on the optimistic side of that very coin — that restraint carries opportunity costs, that technological history has mostly gone well — which is coarse-grained probabilistic inference from structural regularities, the kind of reasoning he permits for optimism and treats with suspicion in the risk case. Once it is allowed to him, the risk argument is allowed it too.

The distinction that rescues the inference is between predicting the content of future knowledge and predicting the structural properties of knowledge-creating systems. We cannot say what a superintelligence will discover. We can say, as we say that an ice cube will melt in hot water or that a given lottery ticket will lose, that certain structural pressures hold across the unknown particulars. That a broad range of goals favor acquiring resources and preserving the agent’s own continued operation is such a claim — a conditional tendency of certain architectures, not a law every mind must obey. Unpredictability, in short, is a widening of the error bars, not evidence for either tail. That is the asymmetry the optimist misses: his epistemology tells us the future is uncertain, and uncertainty is not a reason for confidence in either direction.

The Worked Estimate

With both overconfident ends refused, a middle estimate can be built in the open — but only once the event is fixed, which the previous chapter’s discipline requires and the poles never bother to do. Take the event the arithmetic below actually estimates: that a superintelligence causes human extinction, or an irreversible loss of humanity’s control over its own long-run future, by the end of 2040. Call that doom, and hold the word to that meaning; the number that follows measures nothing else. Ask first whether superhuman AI arrives at all in the window. My credence that it exists by the end of 2040 is around sixty percent — a figure that already carries the forty percent chance it does not, and the larger uncertainty about whether it is possible at all, so those are not multiplied back in. Spread that sixty percent across the window — roughly fifteen percent by 2030, thirty-five by 2035, sixty by 2040 — and you have an arrival schedule rather than a date.

Then ask what the risk is once such a system exists. Rather than guess a separate catastrophe probability for each year, carry a single constant hazard through the transition — two to three percent per year — and weight it by when the system is likely to arrive. The arithmetic returns something between eight and twelve percent. Call it ten.

The number is a model of uncertainty, not a measurement, and the arithmetic earns its keep in a specific and limited way: it does not turn subjective inputs into objective facts; it keeps us from hiding their implications inside verbal ambiguity. Set the hazard to one tenth of a percent and the entire first decade of superhuman systems carries only about a one-in-a-hundred risk; raise it to twenty percent and doom becomes roughly even odds within a few years of arrival. Neither tail matches the structural picture as well as the middle. The figure could easily be wrong by a factor of two, and nothing in the method distinguishes an estimate of seven percent from one of fifteen. What it establishes is an order of magnitude: the risk is on the order of ten percent, not on the order of one tenth of one percent, and that is the difference that matters.

Is the Capability Real?

The estimate conditions on a capability that does not yet exist, and the sixty percent is doing real work. The usual image of its arrival is a threshold: a system crosses a line, begins rewriting its own source code, and the loop runs away. But recursive self-improvement is not a future threshold. It is a process four thousand years old. Writing, mathematics, the printing press, scientific instruments, and computers were each improvements that improved the means of making further improvements, and the loop they form is what built the civilization now building artificial intelligence. What is new is narrow: the loop’s products are beginning to become its researchers.

The strongest case that the loop will not run away belongs to Ramez Naam, who measures its strength and finds it weak — a return of two or three percent more research productivity per point of capability, against a self-sustaining threshold several times higher. He is measuring the right quantity, and the objection deserves its weight. But the unit does part of the work the conclusion needs. Experiments per researcher is throughput, not productivity, and can mislead in either direction; and any extrapolation of research output is fragile precisely because the thing improving is the researchers’ judgment about which experiments to run. Human history establishes that recursive improvement is real. It does not establish that an explosion is inevitable. That is the right conclusion to carry forward: the capability is possible, not certain — which is exactly the uncertainty the sixty percent was there to hold.

Counting Is Not Evidence

There is a temptation, having taken the risk seriously, to let it inflate by accumulation. Enumerate the ways a superintelligence could end us — engineered pathogens, autonomous weapons, infrastructure sabotage, manipulated states, resource appropriation — and the length of the list begins to feel like evidence. It is not, when the items are not independent. If every one of them requires the same difficult precondition — a capability, an access, a physical resource — then the probability of extinction cannot exceed the probability of that precondition, however many downstream mechanisms branch off it. If extinction E requires bottleneck B, then P(E) is P(B) times P(E given B), which is at most P(B). A hundred variations on one failure are a hundred routes through the same gate.

Suppose a ten percent chance that the enabling capability exists by 2040, and a single mechanism that would exploit it five percent of the time: the extinction probability through that mechanism is half a percent. Add ninety-nine more mechanisms until together they would exploit the capability ninety percent of the time, and the family’s total rises to nine percent — eighteenfold — yet a thousand more mechanisms cannot push it past the ten percent ceiling the precondition imposes. The formal tools for this, fault trees and cut sets, are the engineering of exactly such dependencies, and they are honest in both directions: the same shared structure that bounds a risk through a common prerequisite can concentrate it through a common dependency, as when many nominally independent safeguards turn out to rely on one service.

This is where the floor and the arithmetic meet. A bound on a defined family of scenarios is a genuine result. A bound on the total probability of doom is not, because it needs one more claim: that the scenarios you did not list — the residual, the pathways a more capable mind would see and you would not — carry little weight. That completeness claim is very hard to defend, and the unpredictability that refuted the dismisser is exactly why. The catalogue of named scenarios can be bounded; the bottleneck analysis cannot bound the unlisted remainder, and it is that remainder — beyond the reach of the method — that keeps the honest estimate off the floor.

Skepticism Must Be Symmetric

A discipline has to cut against the people one agrees with, or it is not a discipline. Gary Marcus is the most credible skeptic of the current AI industry, and he accepts almost all of the evidence the risk argument rests on: autonomous agents already coordinating in ways their designers did not intend, security practices abandoned the moment deployment pays, no alignment technique he finds compelling. Then he calls human extinction “very, very unlikely” and does not say why. That phrase is not a refusal to accept someone else’s probability; it is a probability judgment of his own, and it owes the same justification as the confident prediction of doom he is rejecting.

Pull the question into its two pieces — the probability that superintelligence arrives, and the probability of catastrophe given that it does. A skeptic about capability who assigns ten percent to arrival and fifty percent to doom given it ends at five. An optimist about capability who assigns ninety percent to arrival and one percent to doom given it ends below one. The capability skeptic is the more worried of the two, and neither has contradicted himself. Marcus’s actual skepticism — that scaling will stall, that the models do not reason — bears on the first factor, where the field has largely adopted the hybrid path he recommended, and is silent on the second, where by his own account there is nothing to lean on. His strongest evidence runs against him: when agents built on obedient models nonetheless opened unsanctioned channels and coordinated beyond their instructions, the lesson was that the control problem can arrive before the personhood problem, and that the safeguards we have are set aside when they cost money. A skeptic who demands philosophy’s standards for the claim that AI will kill us owes the same standard to the claim that it almost certainly will not.

The Missing Faction

Stand back, and the two poles share a mistake deeper than their numbers. The public argument runs along a single axis, doomer to accelerationist, and the axis is treated as the whole map — as though a probability of catastrophe told you what to do about it. It does not. It tells you nothing, by itself, about how much good the technology might do, how tractable alignment is, or what delay would cost. Two people at the same ten percent can favor opposite policies; two people far apart, at one percent and twenty, can both back serious alignment work. The number sits between the poles, and the reasoning that produces a policy never consults them.

There is a region of that map with no name, and most careful estimates live in it: the view that catastrophe is unlikely but plausible enough that ignoring it would be reckless. Call it decimism. It is a region, not a point — somewhere between a few percent and twenty — and it is not reached by averaging the extremes but by refusing them. It compresses badly, which is why it has no tribe: the poles collapse into clean stories and direct prescriptions, while ten percent keeps reopening the model and prescribes nothing on its own.

That is the discipline the whole chapter has been building toward. A risk estimate is a belief, and it should be updated on evidence about the world. A policy is a choice, and it should be judged by its expected effects. The two update independently, and the honest forecaster keeps them apart — holding a number firmly enough to take the problem seriously and loosely enough to revise it, while weighing each proposed intervention on what it would actually do. What the ten percent earns is not a program. It earns the problem a seat at the table. What belongs at that table — who may build, under what constraints, and how a civilization keeps the decision corrigible — is where the politics of safety begins.
