# Cross-Volume Audit: Moral Pressure and Extortion Comparisons

## Scope

This audit tests the manuscript's slavery, mugging, extortion, and protection-racket comparisons against the operational definitions in:

- Volume 5 Chapter 9 for coercion;
- Volume 5 Chapter 11 for harm; and
- Volume 7 Chapter 4 for extortion-funded organizations.

## Ownership rule

Coercion is the deliberate use of a credible conditional threat of harm to obtain compliance. Influence, criticism, guilt, shame, reputational argument, and refusal to associate are not coercion by definition. They cross the line only where a speaker conditions a credible material penalty they control on compliance.

Extortion and protection rackets inherit that structure. Their mechanism requires a demand, a credible conditional threat, control or operational participation in the threatened setback, and sought compliance or payment. The labels may not serve as generic synonyms for hypocrisy, pressure, or moral disapproval.

Comparisons with slavery must state the shared dimension and preserve differences in bodily ownership, inherited status, domination, exit, legal standing, and violence.

## Findings

### Volume 7 Chapters 3–5: retain

The coercion continuum declares one partial axis — compelled extraction — and repeatedly denies moral equivalence between taxation and chattel slavery. The EFO chapter supplies the threat-and-payment mechanism and explicitly separates classification from condemnation. The Grey Zone owns justification. These comparisons remain sharp but bounded.

### Volume 5 Chapter 15: revise

The chapter's non-aid argument correctly denies that need alone creates a claim. Its “emotional currency” paragraph nevertheless called moral pressure coercion by definition and equated threats to standing or self-image with a mugger's bodily threat. That contradicted both the canonical coercion definition and the chapter's later statement that condemnation is an expression of the critic's agency rather than a binding claim.

The revision keeps the title and the mugger comparison where the structure is earned. Moral language becomes coercive when a speaker conditions firing, exclusion from a necessary service, coordinated blacklisting, or another material deprivation they control on compliance. Manufactured guilt, criticism, conscience, reputation, shame, and voluntary nonassociation remain influence unless that credible conditional threat is present.

### Volume 8 Chapter 13: revise

The phrase “running a protection racket on its own principles” named inconsistency without any demand, payment, or threatened setback. It now says the culture is betraying its principles. The chapter's separate claims about labels and agency remain intact.

### Other comparisons: retain

Conditionalism's slavery example identifies the evaluative and factual premises of condemnation. Volume 5's later enslavement examples concern instrumental control of an agent. Volume 7's law, communism, and political-program chapters name distinct mechanisms. Volume 8 Chapter 25 separates Atlantic slavery's specific institutions from both wider history and present remedies. None needs the full comparison repeated or compressed.

## Decision

Preserve the manuscript's confrontational comparisons where their shared mechanism is stated. Do not let *mugging*, *extortion*, *protection racket*, or *slavery* substitute emotional force for the distinctions the argument requires.
