# Cross-Volume Audit: Force and Coercion Under Fire

## Scope

This audit tests the trolley and Burning Hospital family against the operational distinctions owned by Volume 5:

- Chapter 9: coercion as a credible conditional threat used to obtain compliance;
- Chapter 11: material harm relative to a baseline;
- Chapter 12: direct force and coercion as distinct mechanisms;
- Chapter 20: prospective exposure as harm; and
- Chapters 24–25: stress tests and the unresolved innocent-shield contradiction.

## Ownership rule

Coercion, direct force, restraint, imposed risk, and realized injury can overlap, but none is a synonym for the others.

- A conditional threat used to obtain compliance can be coercion before force occurs.
- Locking, shoving, striking, or confining can be direct force or restraint without any demand.
- Materially worsened exposure can be prospective harm without coercion or realized injury.
- Consent can authorize restraint or risk without making the physical act disappear.
- Authorization and justification affect wrongfulness; they do not rewrite the mechanism.

## Findings

### Chapters 20 and 25: retain

Chapter 20 owns prospective harm and explicitly says it is coercive only when used as a conditional threat for compliance. Chapter 25 recaps the Burning Hospital only to state the harder invariant before exposing its conflict with the moral-debt answer. The recap preserves consent, protection, necessity, proportionality, and the open contradiction rather than re-running the case.

### Chapter 24: revise

The chapter states the canonical threat requirement, but three later labels collapsed adjacent mechanisms:

- post-hoc compensation for imposed injury was called extortion despite containing no demand;
- consensual locking was described as “no longer coercion,” implying that restraint itself had been coercion;
- nonconsensual shoving into danger was called coercion and the actor a coercive predator despite no conditional threat for compliance.

The revision leaves every verdict intact. Post-hoc compensation does not authorize instrumental harm. Informed consent can authorize restraint. Pulling someone toward safety can be protective force; shoving them into greater danger can be wrongful force and imposed harm. A threat such as “help us or we'll hurt you” remains coercion because it supplies the missing conditional demand.

### Other appearances: retain

Chapter 22's list of trolley, shield, organ, and hospital cases previews the stress-test jurisdiction in one sentence. The volume introduction names the part's destination. No later political chapter reruns the cases.

## Decision

Keep the full trolley and Burning Hospital analysis in Volume 5 Chapter 24 and the open contradiction in Chapter 25. Preserve the firewall: direct force can be wrongful without being coercion, and coercion requires a credible conditional threat used for compliance.
