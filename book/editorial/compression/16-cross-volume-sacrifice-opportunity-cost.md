# Phase 7 Cross-Volume Sacrifice/Opportunity-Cost Audit

Baseline: July 26, 2026, after the no-value-without-a-valuer audit.

## Scope

This audit tests the repetition-ledger instruction to bridge later uses of sacrifice and opportunity cost to Volume 5 Chapter 4 without turning behavior into privileged access to an undistorted preference ledger.

The canonical chapter makes a deliberately evidential claim. Costly, informed, voluntary choice can support an inference about comparative valuation. The inference remains sensitive to perceived alternatives, duress, addiction, compulsion, misinformation, manipulated defaults, poverty, dependence, adaptive preference, temporal instability, and strategic nonpayment. Avowals, attention, regret, attempts at self-control, and patterns across time can supply additional evidence.

The audit searched the manuscript for sacrifice, opportunity cost, revealed preference, willingness to pay, costly signals, staking, attention, and self-assessed valuation, then read the Volume 6 Chapters 1–5 and 15–18 sequences and the Volume 9 Chapters 2, 9, and 10 applications continuously. The ownership test asked:

1. Is a later cost an agent's voluntary tradeoff, an imposed loss, a market price, or the production cost of a signal?
2. What proposition does the behavior support: comparative value, underlying capacity, a declared reservation price, a forecast, or commitment?
3. Are the agent's knowledge, alternatives, resources, incentives, and constraints visible?
4. Does the passage claim evidence, or does it overreach to a complete or “true” valuation?

## Canonical ownership

Volume 5 Chapter 4 remains intact. It owns the behavioral-evidence claim, the willed-and-able test, opportunity cost, distortion conditions, the free-rider qualification, and the distinction between subjective value and observable sacrifice. Its conclusion states the governing limit: sacrifice is evidence with conditions, not a verdict that bypasses interpretation.

The glossary retains the compact definition and the same warning about duress, addiction, misinformation, poverty, and impaired capacity.

## Corrected mechanism claims

Volume 6 Chapter 17 applied cost-bearing reports to Harberger taxation and insurance but described the resulting number as a “true valuation,” “your truth,” and society's first honest measure of intangible wealth. Those claims exceeded what the mechanism can elicit.

The revision preserves the proposed mechanisms while naming their outputs precisely:

- A Harberger tax can pressure a self-assessed sale price toward an actionable reservation price under specified tax rates, forced-sale rules, liquidity, transaction costs, and risk tolerance.
- A coverage declaration can reveal an insurable valuation shaped by income, liquidity, risk tolerance, beliefs about loss, premiums, and coverage limits.
- Pre-commitment may narrow later argument, but causation, scope, fraud, the occurrence of a covered loss, and nonfinancial meaning can remain outside the number.
- Prediction-market prices remain conditional signals whose strength depends on resolution, participation, liquidity, incentives, and safeguards.

The mechanism makes some directional distortions costly. It does not disclose an owner's complete preference ordering or a context-free truth.

**Decision:** Correct the overclaim; retain the mechanism proposal and its prediction-market qualifications.

## Added applied-volume bridge

Volume 9 Chapter 9 uses foregone alternatives to explain why reversible, costless choices supply little evidence of commitment under affluence. The passage now links that inference to the canonical chapter and explicitly calls the evidence behavioral. Its later distinction between authored constraint and imposed deprivation already prevents the application from romanticizing poverty or suffering.

**Decision:** Add one compact bridge; retain the existential application.

## Retained applications

### Markets and attention

Volume 6 Chapter 1 provides the applied volume's single qualified bridge: voluntary sacrifice is evidence of a preference ordering but is inconclusive under duress, compulsion, misinformation, poverty, and constrained alternatives. It then adds tradeoffs, numeraires, and the limits of scalar representation. Chapters 2–4 distinguish value from wealth, price, and funding success. Chapter 5 treats attention as allocation with opportunity cost while expressly preserving salience capture, addiction, threat, habit, compulsion, misinformation, and platform design as distortions.

These passages add economic quantities and failure modes rather than repeating the canonical chapter.

**Decision:** Retain.

### Incentives, credentials, and reports

Volume 6 Chapter 15 concerns incentive gradients that make strategies attractive, not proof of an agent's reflective values. Chapter 16 concerns costly signals of underlying capacity: a peacock's tail or credential can be hard to fake under a signaling equilibrium without revealing what the signaler values. Chapter 17 designs consequences for valuation and forecast reports. Chapter 18 asks whether institutional incentives and unavailable alternatives compromise consent.

The shared language of cost and signal does not make these one inference. Comparative valuation, capacity evidence, report elicitation, epistemic calibration, and consent remain distinct.

**Decision:** Retain, with the Chapter 17 corrections above.

### Sacred commitment and chosen struggle

Volume 9 Chapter 2 studies what an agent protects from ordinary tradeoff and warns that observed refusal does not prove the commitment rational, good, or beyond review. Chapter 9 applies foregone alternatives to chosen struggle under affluence while rejecting imposed scarcity as a gift. Chapter 10 declares the commitments the author is willing to organize a life around rather than deriving them from cost alone.

These passages use action to make commitment legible without reducing sacred or existential value to price.

**Decision:** Retain.
